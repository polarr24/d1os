## 1. THE ONE THAT MATTERS MOST — your 4.9 is probably a 5.2

Hand timing reads **0.22–0.26 seconds faster than electronic**. That is measured, on 81
NCAA Division I football players: novice hand timers averaged 4.86, experienced 4.90,
electronic 5.12. Self-timing — you or a friend starting and stopping the clock by eye —
is worse still, because the same person anticipates both ends.

**Your 4.9 was self-timed. On a laser you are most likely 5.15–5.30.**

This is not bad news. Three things follow from it:

- The room to improve is **bigger** than the app thought.
- The first number that matters is one somebody else records. Your cousin with a
  stopwatch is closer to real than you with a phone, and a track meet is better than both.
- **Anything under 0.14 seconds is not a change.** Typical error is 0.05–0.06s and the
  smallest worthwhile difference is ~0.12s on a laser, ~0.14s by hand. A retest that comes
  back 0.07 faster has told you nothing.

## 2. SPEED — what actually moves a 40, ranked by evidence

From a systematic review and meta-analysis of sprint training in football-code athletes:

| Method | Effect on 0–10 m |
|---|---|
| Sprinting alone (in athletes who already sprint) | no significant effect |
| **Resisted sprints (sled)** | **0.52** |
| **Strength / plyometrics** | **0.56** |
| **Both combined** | **0.60** |
| Sprint + sled together | **0.96** |

**The "sprinting alone doesn't work" result almost certainly does not apply to you.** Those
athletes already touch top speed in their sport. RB and MLB reps are 0–15 yard bursts with
a blocker in the way — you are probably never reaching true max velocity. That is inference,
not evidence: nobody has run this study on sprint-naive American football freshmen.

**Sled loading — the part everyone gets wrong.** The "10% of bodyweight" rule you will hear
at the gym is far too light to change anything. The threshold where resisted sprints start
beating unresisted ones is around **20% of bodyweight**, and in a study of high-school
athletes the *heaviest* group — running at a quarter of normal speed — improved most
(d = 0.84–1.16). Prescribe it with a stopwatch, not with plates: time a 10-yard sprint
empty, then add weight until the same 10 yards takes about a third longer. Re-check monthly
and whenever the surface changes.

**Dose.** "Three max-velocity runs a week" is a coaching heuristic, not a finding — I traced
it, and the article that popularised it cites no source for the number. What is supported:
one properly rested quality session per week moved a 30-metre time **0.18s in ten weeks**,
and splitting the same volume across two sessions was no better.

**Mechanics.** The universal cue is "lean forward" and it is wrong. Shank angle at touchdown
correlates **r = −0.76** with how much of your force goes horizontally. Trunk lean correlates
with nothing. Bending at the waist is the most common way to look fast and be slow. Cue the
ground, not yourself — "push the ground away" beat "drive your legs" by g = 0.28, and the
effect was biggest in athletes who had never been coached on it.

**Arms matter less than folklore says.** Fully restricting arm motion slowed a 30-metre
sprint by 1.6% — under a tenth of a second. Don't spend coaching time there.

**Realistic improvement:** 3–6% off an electronic 40 in six months (0.15–0.30s), 5–10% in
twelve (0.25–0.50s). That is a real, large gain. It is not 4.9 becoming 4.5.

## 3. STRENGTH — you are just below the ceiling, which is good news

Relative squat strength tracks sprint performance until roughly **2.0× bodyweight**, above
which the relationship flattens. At 275–315 lb and 165 lb bodyweight you are at
**1.67–1.91×**. You are under it. More strength should still make you faster.

The trap: you are gaining weight on purpose, so **315 at 175 lb is a worse ratio than 315
at 165**. The bar has to outrun the scale.

Your bench and deadlift are largely irrelevant to the 40 — no evidence base connects
upper-body pressing to sprint speed. They matter for contact. They are not speed work.

**Your instinct about lower-body power is correct and the evidence backs it.**

## 4. PROJECT HULK — the honest version

You said "bigger than humanly possible." At 5'9" that target would cost you the thing you
are chasing, because fat mass is a pure penalty on every sprint measure and speed is the gap
you named twice.

**There is no weight at which a running back suddenly gets slow.** I looked for a threshold
and none exists in the literature. What exists is a mechanism: mass is free if it is lean,
sits on your hips and thighs, and comes with proportional strength. It costs you the moment
relative strength falls.

So the target is: **carry the most mass you can without losing a step** — and that has a
weekly pass/fail that "as big as possible" never will.

**Three numbers, all three or none:**
- bodyweight **climbing**
- squat ÷ bodyweight **holding or rising**
- vertical **holding or rising**

**Rate:** 0.5–1 lb/week for a teenage male is the lean band; anything faster is fat or water.
To reach 178 by next August you need **0.34 lb/week** — comfortably inside it. Nothing has to
be forced.

**Why this is a D1 argument, not vanity:** sprint *momentum* (mass × velocity) discriminates
senior from junior players in the research where raw velocity does not. For a short back,
momentum is the case.

**The honest gap in your habits:** you eat "randomly" and weigh yourself "sometimes,
whenever." Both of those are free to fix and both are worth more than any supplement. Same
scale, same time, same day each week — otherwise the number is noise, exactly like the 40.

## 5. CLEATS AND TURF — one minute, tonight

You play **and** practise on artificial turf. That matters more than it sounds.

Natural grass tears and releases a planted foot. **Artificial turf does not.** So on turf the
stud pattern is the entire release mechanism, and the wrong one keeps your foot fixed while
your knee keeps rotating.

- **Bladed / knife studs** show significantly higher odds of injury — they resist rotation.
- **Rounded or triangular studs** on a non-aggressive firm-ground plate are what the
  literature recommends for artificial grass.
- Six-stud plates are specifically called out as unsuitable for AG.

**Turn your cleats over and look.** That is the whole action.

Your **mid-cut is already the right cut** for a running back — position guidance is low or
mid, for the ankle mobility a cut needs. Nothing to change there.

*Caveat, stated plainly:* most of this evidence comes from soccer and from female athletes,
where ACL rates are highest. The mechanism carries. The effect size for a 15-year-old male
running back has not been measured.

## 6. FILM — what Hudl itself says

- **3–5 minutes maximum.** Not longer.
- **Title card first:** name, position, jersey number, graduation year, contact.
- **Your top 10 plays from the whole season next**, 30–45 seconds. Then 3–4 skill sections.
- **Spotlight yourself** with a shadow or arrow, but don't let the animation take over.
- **"There is no guarantee a coach will watch your full reel — grab their attention in the
  first 30 seconds."**
- Do **not** open with stats or achievements. Show a variety of skills, not just scores.

**Your situation:** the school films every game to Hudl. The footage probably exists. What
does not exist is your access and your knowledge of which plays are yours. That is a
thirty-minute job — a login and one watch-through — not a two-hour one.
